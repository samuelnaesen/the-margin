III. On Margins

A page is not filled; it is framed. The careful compositor begins with the margins, and only afterwards with the words.

Divide the leaf into nine parts, both across and down. Give one part to the inner margin and one to the head. Give two to the outer margin and two to the foot. What remains is the text area, and it keeps the very proportions of the page itself.

This rule is old. It was not invented so much as found, the way one finds a path with one’s feet before one finds it on a map.

The outer margin is the widest because the eye must rest somewhere, and because readers have always used it: for sums, for objections, for prayers.

Study the example in plate B, a page of 1491 whose margins are crowded with the notes of four owners. Each wrote as if alone. None was.

A margin is where a book is answered. The compositor who leaves too little of it has decided, without asking, that no one will ever reply.

Leave room, then. Someone is always reading over your shoulder, and sooner or later they will want to say something back.

IV. On Ink

Good ink sits on the paper; poor ink sinks into it. The first is read, the second is merely seen.

There are inks that do neither. Lemon, milk, and certain salts dry to nothing and wait. Warmth brings them back, browned and faint, as if the page were remembering something it had been told not to say.

Such inks have no place in honest printing.

Order matters as much as ink. A book bound out of sequence is not a book but a heap, and the folio, the humble page number, is all that stands between them.

Check every folio before the sheets go to the binder. A reader forgives a misspelling. A reader does not forgive being lost.

When in doubt, count. The numbers will tell you where you are, even when the words will not.

V. On Authorship

The compositor sets another’s words, letter by letter, until his hands know them better than the author does.

By the end of a long book it can be hard to say whose hand set which line. This is not a fault. It is what working closely does.

Two hands on one page, given long enough, begin to keep the same time.

It wants a word. Four letters. I think I already know it.
